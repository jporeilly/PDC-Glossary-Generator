Implementing a business glossary

A catalog gives you containers. It does not give you conventions. This is the rollout, the conditions under which glossaries drift, and the gotchas that look like success while being wrong.

Premise Why this exists

The governed unit is the row, not the glossary

Pentaho Data Catalog ships everything needed to hold a governed glossary: glossaries, business terms, tags, sensitivity, roles, and Data Identification methods that stamp terms onto columns automatically. What it does not do — what no catalog does — is force two people to describe the same concept the same way.

That matters because the same three facts about a column get decided in more than one place, and nothing reconciles them:

FactDecided in the glossaryDecided again in…
What the concept isBusiness term and definitionAn identification method bound to a term
How it is labelledTags on the termTags a pattern or dictionary stamps on match
How sensitive it isSensitivity on the termSensitivity applied by identification

Six months on you have PII and pii, "Customer ID" and "Cust Identifier", one steward calling a postcode HIGH and another calling it LOW — and a classification you cannot defend in an audit. That is not a discipline failure. It is the predictable result of asking people to hold a convention in their heads across ten domains and two years.

So the governed unit is not the glossary. It is one row per concept — the term, its controlled tags, its rule-derived sensitivity, its category — written once and read by everything downstream. That row is the Registry. The glossary export is an output; the Registry is the asset.

Drift Four conditions

Why glossaries drift

Drift is not random. It is what happens when any one of the following is missing, and each has a symptom you can recognise before the damage is expensive.

X

A controlled tag vocabulary

Unless tags come from an allow-list, every steward invents their own. Free-text tagging produces PII, pii, PII-Data, personal — four labels, one concept, and a search that returns a quarter of what it should.

TELL — tag counts keep rising while coverage doesn't.

Y

Deterministic classification

Unless sensitivity and PII category are derived from evidence by rule, they become opinion — and opinion varies by person, by mood, and by how much time someone had that afternoon. Let a language model decide them and they vary per run, which means the classification is not reproducible and therefore not auditable.

Do NOT return sensitivity or PII — those are deterministic from the scan.

The model prompt, Glossary Generator

A model is a drafting aid for language — names, definitions, purpose. It gets no vote on classification.

TELL — two runs over the same data return different sensitivity.

A live specimen: a water utility's ph_level column was expanded by a generic built-in abbreviation to "Phone Level" — and the PII name-matcher then read the expanded name, stamping CONTACT_INFO, contact and privacy tags, and MEDIUM sensitivity onto a chemistry measurement. One wrong generic assumption, three governance fields corrupted, everything looking deliberate. The cure was not a better guess but no guess: ambiguous tokens get no global opinion, and the company's own pack records the right expansion the first time a steward renames the term.

Z

One row read by both the glossary and the methods

Unless the glossary and the detection methods draw from the same row, they diverge the moment either is edited. Someone tightens a pattern; the term's tags no longer match what the pattern stamps. Nothing errors. The catalog is now quietly inconsistent — which is worse than being loudly wrong, because nobody goes looking.

TELL — a column carrying a term whose tags differ from the term's own.

+

Visibility across glossaries

Once you have several glossaries, each steward re-authors concepts their neighbours already own. "Customer Identifier" in Customer, "Cust ID" in Billing, "Client Number" in Servicing — three terms, one concept, and no way to roll up.

TELL — the same concept resolves to a different term id per domain.

Rollout Standards first

The order matters more than the pace

00 Pilot one domain

Pick one domain with a willing steward and genuinely important data. Run it end to end. The deliverable is not the glossary — it is the convention: the domain pack, the tag dictionary, the category set, the sensitivity rules. Budget for this taking longer than everything that follows. It should.

01 Freeze the global layer

Before a second domain starts, fix what every domain shares.

Global — set once, changed by exceptionPer domain — the steward's call
Tag allow-listWhich concepts exist
Sensitivity rulesDefinitions and purpose
Category setStewardship and ratings
Naming conventionsThe approval gate

A steward decides what the concepts are. They do not get to invent the vocabulary those concepts are described in. That single distinction is what makes many small glossaries safe instead of a drift factory.

02 Roll out by domain

One glossary per accountable steward, not per source — sources cut across domains, stewardship doesn't. Scope each load to one coherent subject area so the review stays reviewable.

Per domain: scan and profile, generate, check for existing terms, review, govern, resolve, apply. Run the cross-glossary check early on later domains — reuse climbs as coverage grows, and reusing is far cheaper than reconciling.

03 Deploy methods, then drift-check

Feed the Registry to the Policy Generator, deploy the dictionaries and patterns, then run its drift check on a schedule. This is the step that makes the glossary operational rather than documentation: terms start landing on columns automatically, stamped with the tags the Registry says they carry.

Sizing The domain split

How many glossaries, and how big

  • One glossary per accountable owner. If nobody's name is on it, it decays.
  • Five to fifteen suits a mid-size enterprise. Fewer and review is unmanageable; more and you fragment concepts across too many owners.
  • Terms plateau, columns don't. Sixty customer_id columns collapse into one term carrying sixty sources. An organisation has a few hundred business concepts however many tables express them — size the effort on concepts, not columns.
The pack Author one thing

Building the domain pack

The pack is what makes the second scan visibly better than the first: the vocabulary of one company, learned once. The temptation is to sit down and author an industry taxonomy before scanning anything. Resist it.

table_category / table_terms / terms / tag_rules left EMPTY on purpose. Inventing table names for a database nobody has scanned yet produces rules that never match and read as if they were curated.

The scaffolder's own reasoning

That last clause is the trap. A hand-authored taxonomy looks authoritative, matches nothing, and goes unquestioned because it looks deliberate.

Author one thing: the category list

It is the only input a person reliably knows before scanning, because it comes from how the business talks rather than how its data is modelled. Everything derivable is then derived from it.

  • Five to nine categories. Fewer and they stop discriminating; more and nobody holds the set in their head.
  • Named as the business names them, not as the schema does. Categories mirroring source systems produce a glossary that documents the database rather than the business.
  • One steward per category where you can manage it.

A live specimen — the same folder, twice: two runs of the AI categoriser over the same water-utility scan filed the correspondence folder first under Records & Documents — the records-management lens: what kind of artifact this is, where retention lives — then under Communications & Outreach — the function lens: what the business does with it. Same evidence, same schema; two defensible taxonomies. Neither is wrong: abstraction has degrees of freedom, and every run samples one. Ask the same consultant twice, get two org charts. That is category drift in its purest form, and the cure is not a better model — it is a decision that sticks: proposals land as pills, the steward picks a lens once, and Export pack freezes the choice so every later run proposes against a settled baseline instead of renaming it.

The abbreviations earn the most and are the least guessable: every organisation shortens words its own way, and that mapping is exactly what makes the next scan arrive with mbr_no already reading as Member Number.

Two rules keep it safe. Only approved vocabulary reaches the pack, so it inherits the steward's judgement rather than the scanner's guesses. And merges surface conflicts side by side instead of overwriting — a steward's recorded decision beats the machine's newest opinion.

Industry standards are a check, never a seed

AWWA for water, FIBO or BIAN for financial services and their equivalents elsewhere are useful after a first pass, as a diff. Seed from one and you import vocabulary the company does not actually use — you end up governing terms nobody says out loud, which is how a glossary becomes shelfware. Run the domain, then compare against the standard to find the gaps that are genuinely missing rather than merely absent.

scaffold → scan → review → export pack → commit → next scan starts here

A company with no pack at all is a legitimate start: run packless, do one full cycle, and the first export is your base pack — built entirely from evidence someone has already approved.

Gotchas Looks like success

Six ways to be wrong quietly

Scanning is not profiling

A file scan defaults withProfile: false and headerExists: false. Inherit those and your CSVs are catalogued with no columns — or columns named Column-0 … Column-N, because the header row was read as data. Every status still reads OK.

Re-profiling is additive

Run a scan again with different header settings and the catalog adds the new columns without retiring the old, leaving Column-0, asset_id, Column-1, asset_type interleaved. Recreate the data source rather than re-running over the top.

Classification before the glossary is a catch-22

Data Classification assigns business terms — which do not exist until the glossary is built, from the very profile the scan produces. Enabled on a first pass it can only mark everything unclassified, leaving a pile to resolve by hand. Run it later, deliberately, and only over unstructured documents; structured files never need it, because their terms are assigned directly with profile evidence behind each one.

The internal API routes by hostname

Reached on a bare IP, internal endpoints answer 401 with a perfectly valid token while the public API works fine — so an object-store file scan fails and everything else succeeds. Use the vhost URL.

A gateway refusal is not an auth failure

Where the catalog sits behind a WAF, proxy or zero-trust edge, a browser login does not authenticate a non-browser client. The symptom reads as "authentication failed"; the cause is an edge refusal before the request ever arrived. Check for the gateway's fingerprint before touching realms and passwords.

Nothing tells you a step was skipped

The through-line in all of the above: the failure state and the success state look identical from the outside. Insist that tooling reports what it didn't do — a skipped profile, a truncated scope, an inherited default — because a green tick over an omitted step is the most expensive thing in this list.

Avoid Anti-patterns

Reliable ways to end up with a glossary nobody trusts

  • One enterprise glossary. No owner, unbounded review, every steward editing the same object.
  • Free-text tags. The single fastest route to drift.
  • AI-authored definitions applied unreviewed. The model drafts; the steward is accountable.
  • Classification run before terms exist. Guarantees a backlog of unclassified assets.
  • Per-steward sensitivity judgement. Sensitivity is a rule, not an opinion.
  • Treating the glossary as the deliverable. It is the Registry that keeps everything else honest.
Limits Stated plainly

What tooling does not solve

  • Concurrency. Several stewards working simultaneously need separate instances of the authoring tool.
  • Very large single sources. Profiling scope is bounded per source and reported rather than silently clipped. Row counts are irrelevant — a ten-million-row table is one entity.
  • Long-running scans. A loader that waits synchronously per source suits a demo, not an overnight estate scan.
  • Organisational agreement. No tool decides who owns "Customer". Settle that before the rollout, or the glossaries will encode the argument.
